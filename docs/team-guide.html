Your own research, on our problem

Each of you runs a small research project of your own. You pick the question, find the data, and produce the findings. You report back to Joao, and anything that holds up gets brought into the main project.

The problem we are all working on

NeuroDiffusion tries to turn what someone imagines into an image, using EEG brain recordings and an image generator.

Where it stands: in our current dataset there is a real but weak signal. When people imagine one of 33 pictures, a simple method picks the right one 2 to 3 times more often than random guessing. The biggest thing holding us back is data. Each of our 4 people imagined each picture only once.

So the most useful research finds better data, better ways to clean it, or evidence about what works. It does not have to be big. A clear answer to a small question is worth more than a vague answer to a big one.

Step by step

01Set up, once

Accept the GitHub invite first (check your email or the invitations page), or you won’t be able to push. Then:

git clone https://github.com/JoaoLucasVeras/NeuroDiffusion.git
cd NeuroDiffusion
conda env create -f env.yaml
conda activate neurodiffusion
02Pick a question and tell Joao

One sentence you could answer with yes, no or a number. Tell Joao before starting, so two people don’t chase the same thing. The ideas further down are a starting point, not a list you have to choose from.

03Make your branch and your folder

All your work lives in research/yourname/. Copy the findings template in as your write-up.

git checkout -b yourname/your-topic origin/main
mkdir research/yourname
cp research/TEMPLATE.md research/yourname/FINDINGS.md
git add research/yourname/FINDINGS.md
git commit -m "Start research: your question here"
git push -u origin yourname/your-topic
04Find your data

Check the table of datasets we already reviewed first, so you don’t redo work. Then judge a new one against the four checks below. Put downloaded data in research/yourname/data/, which Git ignores.

05Try the simplest thing first

Before anything fancy, see whether a simple method finds any signal at all. Our code/classical_baseline.py does exactly that. If you convert your dataset into our format, it runs unchanged, and your numbers become directly comparable to ours.

06Write it up and push, every session

Keep FINDINGS.md current and push at the end of each work session, even if it’s unfinished. That’s how Joao follows along without needing a meeting.

07Report to Joao

When you have an answer, even “no”, send Joao your branch name. He reads your write-up and you talk it through.

08If it holds up, bring it into the project

You and Joao decide together what goes in: maybe a new data loader, a cleaning step, or just your write-up added to the project’s resources. Then open a pull request and Joao reviews it.

How to judge a dataset

We have learned the hard way that most EEG datasets cannot help us. These four checks rule out most of them, usually from the description alone.

People imagined imagesmust have

Not just looked at them, and not imagined movements. “Motor imagery” means imagining moving a hand, which has no picture in it.

Electrodes on the back of the headmust have

Imagery signal sits over the back of the head. Look for O1, O2, Oz or PO channels. Many consumer headsets don’t have them.

Real repeatsmust have

Each image or category recorded several separate times per person. Cutting one long recording into pieces doesn’t count.

More than one session

Recordings on different days let us test whether a model survives putting the headset back on. Nice to have.

Ideas to start from

Take one, change one, or bring your own. Anything that connects back to the problem above counts.

  • Is there a visual imagery dataset we’ve missed? Search OpenNeuro, figshare, Zenodo and the “data availability” sections of recent imagery papers.
  • Which headsets could we record with ourselves? Affordable ones with electrodes on the back of the head. This decides whether we can collect our own data.
  • Does cleaning out blinks help? ICA removes eye blinks from a recording without throwing it away. Try it on our data or yours.
  • What do recent papers actually achieve on imagery? And how did they test it? Many published results don’t hold up, and knowing which ones are solid tells us what to aim for.
  • Can data from seeing images help with imagining them? There is far more perception data than imagery data. Is there evidence a model trained on one transfers to the other?
  • How well can we do with only a few categories? Gao et al. reached 75.8% with 3 categories. Would a few categories be a realistic first demo?

Five rules

Stay in your own branch. Everyone can technically push to anyone’s branch, so please don’t push to one with someone else’s name on it.
Never commit data. Keep it in research/yourname/data/, which Git ignores. Removing a committed dataset later means rewriting history for everyone.
Every number comes with its chance level. “9% accuracy” means nothing until you know random guessing gets 3%. If you can, add a p-value from a permutation test. classical_baseline.py --permute 200 does one for you.
Check the license. Note it in your write-up. Some datasets are research-only.
Ask before using the GPU cluster. It’s shared, and most research questions don’t need it.
If a result looks surprisingly good

Be suspicious first and excited second. This field has a long history of exciting results that turned out to be recording noise, and we have already caught one of our own. Bring it to Joao and we will check it together. That is the point of the review, not a judgement on your work.
