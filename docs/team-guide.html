Your own research, on our problem

Each of you runs a small research project of your own. It can be new data, a new way to clean the signal, a new model, or a whole new pipeline. You pick the question and produce the findings. You report back to Joao, and anything that holds up gets brought into the main project.

The problem we are all working on

NeuroDiffusion tries to turn what someone imagines into an image, using EEG brain recordings and an image generator.

Where it stands: in our current dataset there is a real but weak signal. When people imagine one of 33 pictures, a simple method picks the right one 2 to 3 times more often than random guessing. The biggest thing holding us back is data. Each of our 4 people imagined each picture only once.

So useful research can come from anywhere: better data, a better way to process it, a better model, or a better way to measure whether any of it works. It does not have to be big. A clear answer to a small question is worth more than a vague answer to a big one.

Step by step

01Set up, once

Accept the GitHub invite first (check your email or the invitations page), or you won’t be able to push. Then:

git clone https://github.com/JoaoLucasVeras/NeuroDiffusion.git
cd NeuroDiffusion
conda env create -f env.yaml
conda activate neurodiffusion
02Pick a question and tell Joao

One sentence you could answer with yes, no or a number, about any part of the pipeline. Tell Joao before starting, so two people don’t chase the same thing. The ideas further down are a starting point, not a list you have to choose from.

03Make your branch and your folder

All your work lives in research/yourname/. Copy the findings template in as your write-up.

git checkout -b yourname/your-topic origin/main
mkdir research/yourname
cp research/TEMPLATE.md research/yourname/FINDINGS.md
git add research/yourname/FINDINGS.md
git commit -m "Start research: your question here"
git push -u origin yourname/your-topic
04Build it
  • New data: check the datasets we already reviewed, then judge a new one against the four checks below. Put downloads in research/yourname/data/, which Git ignores.
  • New method or pipeline: on your own branch you can change anything, including files in code/. Or write a fresh script in your folder that imports the pieces you want to keep.
05Compare it to what we have

A new idea only counts as better if it beats our current numbers in a fair test. See “Testing a new idea fairly” below. If it’s new data, start with our classical_baseline.py: convert your data into our format and it runs unchanged.

06Write it up and push, every session

Keep FINDINGS.md current and push at the end of each work session, even if it’s unfinished. That’s how Joao follows along without needing a meeting.

07Report to Joao

When you have an answer, even “no”, send Joao your branch name. He reads your write-up and you talk it through.

08If it holds up, bring it into the project

You and Joao decide together what goes in: a new data loader, a cleaning step, a better model, a whole pipeline, or just your write-up added to the project’s resources. Then open a pull request and Joao reviews it.

Testing a new idea fairly

“My version got 9%” tells us nothing on its own. It has to be compared with what we already have, under the same conditions. Otherwise an improvement can’t be told apart from luck or an easier test.

Keep these the same

  • The split. Use datasets/imagination_5_95_std_splits_window_avail.pth, so you train and test on the same recordings we did.
  • The subjects. Report all four people, not just the best one.
  • The test. Score on the test set and run a permutation test (200 shuffles). Our scripts already do this, so reuse their code.
  • Your settings. Choose them before looking at test results. Trying many and keeping the best one makes results look better than they are. We found exactly this bug in our own baseline.

The numbers to beat

Picking the right picture out of 33, as a multiple of random guessing. Alpha band (8–13 Hz).

MethodPerson 1Person 2Person 3Person 4
Simple: signal strength + logistic regression3.25×2.25×2.75×3.00×
Our small neural network (CLIP output)3.25×0.50×2.00×1.75×

The simple method’s p-values are being recalculated after a bug fix, so check with Joao for the latest. Anything that produces CLIP output (step 4) should be compared with the second row, because that is what the image generator needs.

How to judge a dataset

We have learned the hard way that most EEG datasets cannot help us. These four checks rule out most of them, usually from the description alone.

People imagined imagesmust have

Not just looked at them, and not imagined movements. “Motor imagery” means imagining moving a hand, which has no picture in it.

Electrodes on the back of the headmust have

Imagery signal sits over the back of the head. Look for O1, O2, Oz or PO channels. Many consumer headsets don’t have them.

Real repeatsmust have

Each image or category recorded several separate times per person. Cutting one long recording into pieces doesn’t count.

More than one session

Recordings on different days let us test whether a model survives putting the headset back on. Nice to have.

Ideas to start from

Take one, change one, or bring your own. Anything that connects back to the problem above counts. Grouped by pipeline step.

Data

  • Is there a visual imagery dataset we’ve missed? Search OpenNeuro, figshare, Zenodo and the “data availability” sections of recent imagery papers.
  • Which headsets could we record with ourselves? Affordable ones with electrodes on the back of the head. This decides whether we can collect our own data.
  • Can data from seeing images help with imagining them? There is far more perception data than imagery data. Is there evidence a model trained on one transfers to the other?

Cleaning the signal

  • Does removing blinks with ICA help? It cleans a recording without throwing it away, which matters when there are only 33 per person.
  • Which frequencies carry the signal? Compare bands like 1–8, 8–13, 13–30 and 30–50 Hz. If the lowest or highest bands decode well on their own, that is a warning sign of recording noise.
  • Do longer slices help? We use half a second. Imagery papers often use 2 to 4 seconds.

Reading the brain waves

  • Other proven EEG methods. Riemannian geometry, common spatial patterns, or published EEG networks beyond EEGNet.
  • Pretrained EEG models. Large models trained on many EEG datasets are appearing. Do any help on visual tasks with this little data?
  • Data augmentation. Adding small amounts of noise or shifting signals in time to stretch 33 recordings further.

Translating to image numbers, and generating

  • Why does the signal get lost here? The simple method keeps it for all four people, the neural network for one. A different loss function or a smaller output could fix it.
  • Use a generator built for image numbers. Stable Diffusion expects numbers that came from text, and ours come from images. Tools like IP-Adapter take image numbers directly and may fit better.
  • Fewer categories as a first demo. Gao et al. reached 75.8% with 3 categories. Picking a category, then using a known image of it, may be more reliable than generating detail.

Measuring

  • What do recent papers actually achieve on imagery, and how did they test it? Many published results don’t hold up. Knowing which are solid tells us what to aim for.

Six rules

Stay in your own branch. Everyone can technically push to anyone’s branch, so please don’t push to one with someone else’s name on it.
Add, don’t replace, when it comes into the main project. On your branch, change anything. But when shared code comes into the main project, add your version as an option (a new flag or a new script) rather than overwriting the old one. Then our past results can still be reproduced.
Never commit data. Keep it in research/yourname/data/, which Git ignores. Removing a committed dataset later means rewriting history for everyone.
Every number comes with its chance level. “9% accuracy” means nothing until you know random guessing gets 3%. If you can, add a p-value from a permutation test. classical_baseline.py --permute 200 does one for you.
Check the license. Note it in your write-up. Some datasets are research-only.
Ask before using the GPU cluster. It’s shared, and most research questions don’t need it.
If a result looks surprisingly good

Be suspicious first and excited second. This field has a long history of exciting results that turned out to be recording noise, and we have already caught one of our own. Bring it to Joao and we will check it together. That is the point of the review, not a judgement on your work.

Questions go to Joao. The findings template is research/TEMPLATE.md; the full contribution rules are in CONTRIBUTING.md.
