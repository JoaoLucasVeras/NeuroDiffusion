Train on subject 1, test on subject 2, same 33 pictures. Drift cannot transfer between people but imagery content can, so this separates genuine signal from fingerprint. A few CPU-minutes.

Part two

Reference

The standing explanation. This half changes only when our understanding does, not when a run finishes.

Reference 1

The project

The goal is to take a recording of someone's brain activity while they imagine a picture, and produce that picture.

That sentence needs immediate narrowing, because it promises more than is physically possible. We are not reading a picture out of the brain. We extract a weak hint about the category of what someone is thinking about — dog, car, flower — and hand that hint to an image generator that already knows what dogs look like. Nearly all the visual detail in the output comes from the generator's knowledge, not from the brain.

Why this framing matters for every result

The image generator always produces a good-looking picture, whether or not the brain data means anything. So "the output looks like a dog" proves nothing. The only meaningful question is: does the output match the picture the person actually had in mind, more often than it matches some random other picture? Every evaluation in this project is built around that question.

Where the project stands

Perception works. When we feed it EEG recorded while someone is looking at an image, the generated pictures match the real stimulus significantly better than chance pairings — 4.3 standard errors better, and it names the right category out of 40 options 9.5% of the time against 2.5% chance. On images and a person the model had never seen. The machine works.

Imagination does not, yet. The same pipeline on imagination EEG comes back at chance. That is a measured result with working controls, not a crash.

The data

Four people. Each was shown a picture for half a second, then a noise mask, then spent ten seconds imagining that picture with their eyes open. 128 electrodes recorded the whole time.

The single most important fact about this dataset

Each person imagined each picture exactly once. That ten-second recording was later chopped into 20 half-second pieces, so the file looks like it holds 660 examples per person. It holds 33 events, sliced thin. Twenty slices of one event are not twenty measurements.

A comparable modern dataset has around 800 real imagery events per person. We have 33. That ratio governs the ceiling on everything below.

Reference 2

The pipeline

The system has two stages, trained separately.

01 Stage 1 — teach a network the shape of EEG

A masked autoencoder: hide random chunks of an EEG recording and train a network to fill them back in. No labels needed — the recording is its own answer key. The point is to learn what EEG normally looks like, so the next stage starts from something better than random.

02 Stage 2 — steer an image generator with brain signal

Take Stable Diffusion, a system that normally turns text into images, and replace the text input with the EEG encoder from Stage 1. The generator's own image knowledge stays frozen; we train only the parts that translate brain signal into something it understands.

Masked autoencoder

A network trained by hiding part of its input and asking it to reconstruct the hidden part. Because the answer is the input itself, it needs no human labelling, so it can learn from large amounts of raw data.

ExampleCover a quarter of a photograph and ask someone to draw the missing region. To do it well they must learn how photographs generally work — edges continue, textures repeat, shadows fall consistently. That understanding is the real product, not the patch.

Diffusion model

An image generator that starts from pure random static and removes noise in small steps until a picture emerges. What it removes at each step can be steered by a conditioning signal — normally a text prompt, here an EEG recording.

ExampleLike watching a badly tuned TV gradually sharpen into a scene. The conditioning signal is what decides which scene it sharpens into.

CLIP embedding

CLIP is a model that converts an image into a list of about 768 numbers capturing its meaning. Two pictures of different dogs land close together in that space; a dog and a bicycle land far apart. We train the EEG encoder to output numbers close to the CLIP embedding of the picture the person was imagining.

ExampleA coordinate system for meaning. "Golden retriever on grass" sits near "labrador in a park" and far from "red sports car". The EEG encoder's job is to land in the right neighbourhood.

Parameters

The adjustable numbers inside a network, tuned during training. More parameters means more flexibility — and more capacity to memorise instead of learn.

ExampleOur Stage 2 adjusts about 85 million parameters using roughly 360 training examples per subject. That is about 235,000 adjustable knobs per example. A student given 360 practice questions and unlimited memory will memorise the answers rather than learn the subject.

Reference 3

What EEG actually is

An electrode on your scalp measures voltage — the tiny electrical field produced by millions of neurons firing together beneath it. You have 128 of these, each sampled 250 times per second.

That voltage is not "brain activity". It is a sum of several things stacked on top of each other:

What contributesTypical sizeIs it brain?
Brain rhythms (what we want)10–50 µVyes
Eye blinks and eye movement100–200 µVno
Neck and jaw musclevariesno
Heartbeatsmallno
Electrode driftcan be largeno
Mains hum (60 Hz)variesno

Several of the non-brain contributions are larger than the brain signal. That is the basic condition of the field: the thing you want is one of the smaller components of what you record.

Microvolt (µV)

One millionth of a volt. Brain rhythms at the scalp are tens of microvolts. An AA battery is 1.5 volts — roughly fifty thousand times larger.

Frequency is how you pull them apart

Different sources oscillate at different speeds, so you can separate them by frequency — how many times per second the signal cycles up and down. That unit is the hertz (Hz).

below 1 HzDrift — electrodes settling, sweat, temperature. Not brain.
1–4 HzDelta — deep sleep; also where slow artifact lives
4–8 HzTheta — drowsiness, some memory processes
8–13 HzALPHA — the visual cortex idle rhythm. Where imagery lives.
13–30 HzBeta — active concentration, motor control
above 30 HzGamma — often really muscle activity in scalp recordings

Alpha rhythm

Your visual cortex has a resting oscillation near 10 cycles per second. When you see something — or imagine something — that rhythm is modulated in a way that depends on what you are seeing or imagining.

ExampleThree independent studies, including the paper behind our own dataset, found imagined content carried in this band and essentially nowhere else. That is why we filter to 8–13 Hz: it is where theory says the answer is.

Band-pass filter

A processing step that keeps a chosen range of frequencies and discards everything outside it.

ExampleThe bass and treble knobs on a stereo. Band-passing to 8–13 Hz is like turning everything down except one narrow slice of the spectrum — you hear less, but what remains is the part you care about.

Reference 4

The leakage problem

This is the part that makes EEG decoding unusual, and it is the key to understanding our results.

Electrode drift

Over seconds and minutes, the electrical contact between an electrode and your scalp slowly changes — gel settles, skin warms, you shift in the chair. The measured voltage wanders slowly, below 1 Hz. It has nothing to do with thinking.

ExampleCrucially, that wander is unique to that recording session, at that moment, on that person. It is a fingerprint on the data.

Two half-second clips cut from the same ten-second recording share that fingerprint. Clips from a different recording have a completely different one. And a machine learning model will happily learn the fingerprint instead of the thing you wanted it to learn — because the fingerprint is easier.

The smudged lens

Imagine photographing 33 objects, one per day, with a slightly different smudge on your lens each day. Now train a model to answer "which object is this?"

It can learn the smudges rather than the objects. Test it on more photos from those same days and it scores brilliantly. Hand it photographs someone else took and it fails completely — because it never learned what the objects look like.

Why our dataset makes this unavoidable

Each subject imagined each picture exactly once. So there is a perfect one-to-one correspondence:

picture #7  ↔  one 10-second recording  ↔  one unique drift pattern

Which means "identify the recording" and "identify the picture" are the same task. Anything unique to that recording — drift, posture, how the cap was sitting — is a perfect predictor of the label. The model never has to decode imagery at all.

Data leakage

When information from the test set reaches the model during training, making its score look better than its real ability.

ExampleIn ordinary machine learning, leakage is a mistake you avoid. In EEG it is the default state, and you have to deliberately engineer it out. Several well-known EEG image-decoding papers have been invalidated for exactly this.

Split protocol

The rule deciding which trials are used for training and which are held back for testing. In EEG this choice matters more than almost anything else.

ExampleOur window protocol trains on windows 0–10 of each recording and tests on windows 16–19, throwing away windows 11 and 15 as buffers so no training window sits next to a test window. The original paper split those 20 windows randomly, which put a test window's immediate neighbours — nearly identical signal — into training. That is why their number is higher than ours on the same data.

Reference 5

The classical baseline

The problem it exists to solve

Every time the diffusion pipeline came back at chance, that single result could have meant four entirely different things:

  1. There is no imagery signal in this EEG. The data is a dead end.
  2. There is signal, but our model is too large. 85 million parameters, ~360 examples — it memorises instead of learning.
  3. There is signal, but the input representation is wrong. Raw broadband samples when the answer lives in one narrow band.
  4. There is signal and the encoder finds it, but the image-generation stage loses it.

All four produce the identical output — "not distinguishable from chance" — and each diffusion run costs one to two GPU-days while resolving none of them individually. That is an expensive place to be stuck.

What a classical baseline is

A deliberately simple, well-understood method. No diffusion, no image generation, no learned representation. Its power comes from what it cannot do:

  • It cannot memorise. A logistic regression on 128 features has nowhere to hide an answer key for 33 classes.
  • It has no generation stage, so nothing downstream can lose the signal.
  • It runs in minutes on a CPU, not days on a GPU.

So it isolates exactly one question: is there decodable imagery information in this EEG at all? If yes, explanation (1) is eliminated and every remaining failure is ours — which means fixable. If no, we stop optimising a pipeline pointed at noise.

The three methods we ran

Band power + logistic regression

For each electrode, measure how much energy sits in the alpha band, then fit the simplest possible classifier — a weighted sum of those 128 numbers.

ExampleThe direct test of "is alpha power informative?" If this works, the signal is strong and simple.

Riemannian tangent space

Instead of each electrode separately, measure how electrodes co-vary — which pairs rise and fall together. Those relationships form a mathematical object that does not live in ordinary flat space, so it is mapped onto a flat surface ("tangent space") before classification.

ExampleThink of measuring not each musician's volume but which instruments play together. That pattern of coordination is often more informative than the individual levels. This is the standard strong baseline when EEG training data is scarce.

EEGNet

A compact neural network built specifically for EEG — about 2,000 parameters, versus our main model's 85 million. It learns its own spatial and temporal filters.

ExampleThis is the architecture that reached 75.8% on a modern imagery dataset. It is the most flexible of our three methods — which turns out to matter a great deal.

Reference 6

The four conditions

We ran the baseline four ways. Each answers one question.

A Alpha band, 500 ms windows

Keep only 8–13 Hz. Is there imagery signal in the band that theory predicts? This is the real test — the other three are controls around it.

B Broadband, 500 ms windows

Keep everything, 1–50 Hz. Does band-limiting actually help, or was the alpha theory wrong for our data? A control.

C Alpha band, 2-second windows

Glue four adjacent windows together. Is 500 ms too short? At 10 Hz that is only five cycles of the rhythm we are trying to measure — marginal. The imagery literature uses 2–4 seconds.

D Alpha band + artifact rejection

Throw away the 10% most extreme trials, which are mostly blinks. One paper says cleaning hurts decoding; another says it helps in low-signal cases like ours. Cheap enough to settle on our own data.

Why condition C was possible at all

The original 20 windows are non-overlapping and contiguous — they tile the full ten seconds end to end, with no shared samples. So gluing windows 0–3 back together reconstructs the genuine continuous signal from 0 to 2 seconds. Had they overlapped, concatenating would have produced a waveform that never actually happened.

Condition A — the finding

Eleven of twelve results above chance, across four different people and three independent methods, in the band theory predicted. Top-5 accuracy sat at 21–31% against a 15% chance baseline.

Modest. But it says plainly: there is decodable imagery signal in this EEG. Explanation (1) is eliminated.

Condition B — the trap, caught

Broadband roughly doubled every number. That is not extra signal — it is the drift shortcut from Part 4. Three reasons to be confident:

  • It is too good relative to the field. The original paper got 13.4% on 40 options with a leaky split. Subject 1's broadband 37.1% on 33 options would beat everything published.
  • The pattern gives it away. EEGNet — the most flexible method — was the worst on alpha (2.0×) and the best on broadband (12.2×). When your most powerful model gains most from data that shouldn't matter, it is exploiting a shortcut.
  • The mechanism is known. Broadband keeps everything from 1 Hz up, including slow drift. Band-passing to 8–13 Hz filters that drift away — which is exactly why the number drops.

The lower number is the honest one. This is genuinely counterintuitive: removing data made the result worse but more trustworthy. It is also why this field is littered with results that did not replicate.

Condition C — inconclusive

Subjects 2 and 3 improved; subject 4 did not. But gluing windows into 2-second segments leaves only 99 training and 33 test samples, so these numbers bounce around badly. Suggestive, not settled.

Condition D — cleanly negative, and useful

Artifact rejection came out roughly neutral — sometimes up, sometimes down, no consistent direction. That resolves the disagreement between the two papers for our data: it does not matter much here. We can stop spending effort on it, including the artifact-removal papers currently on the reading list.

Reference 8

What this changes

Four things gained

  1. The worst possibility is eliminated. "There is no imagery signal here" is off the table. Every remaining failure points at something we control, and those are fixable.
  2. We have a number to beat. About 3× chance from a model with a few hundred parameters. Any complex model that cannot clear that is not earning its complexity.
  3. We found a trap before it cost weeks. Condition B revealed a shortcut large enough to fake a breakthrough. Finding that in a five-minute CPU job rather than after a month of GPU training is the entire point of the exercise.
  4. We built a reusable instrument. Five CPU-minutes instead of a GPU-week per experiment. This is the tool for everything ahead — and the first thing to run on day one of our own recordings, to answer "is this data any good?" before collecting hundreds of trials on a protocol that might be broken.

The uncomfortable implication

Our Stage 2 deep model trains on raw broadband EEG — exactly the condition that just produced up to 12× chance through drift fingerprinting.

Which means subject 1's headline 26.3% validation score may be substantially drift-driven rather than imagery decoding. That number was already flagged as provisional for other reasons — it is a peak across many training rounds on a lenient measure — but we now have a mechanism for why it might be inflated, and an estimate of the size.

Read correctly, this is the baseline doing its job: it found a problem in the expensive pipeline that the expensive pipeline could not find in itself.

Is this forward or backward?

Forward, decisively — but it is the kind of forward step that makes some earlier numbers look worse. That is what honest instrumentation does. It does not only deliver good news.

The project this morning: an expensive pipeline producing ambiguous nulls, with no way to tell whether the data was the problem. The project now: confirmed signal in the data, a known-size shortcut to avoid, a benchmark to beat, a settled question about artifact removal, and a fast way to test the next idea.

What happens next

  • Feed the pipeline alpha-band input — no longer a speculative improvement but a correction to a known shortcut
  • Run the cross-subject test — all four subjects imagined the same 33 pictures, so training on subject 1 and testing on subject 2 separates genuine content from drift fingerprint. Drift cannot transfer between people; imagery content can
  • Drop artifact removal from the plan — condition D says it is not worth it here
  • Treat every previous broadband result as provisional until re-checked on alpha input
Reference 9

Glossary

Every technical term used in this project, in plain language.

Artifact

Anything in an EEG recording that is not brain activity — blinks, muscle tension, heartbeat, electrode drift, mains hum.

Often larger than the brain signal itself.

Chance level

The accuracy you would get by guessing randomly. With 33 possible answers, that is 1/33 = 3.03%.

Every accuracy number is meaningless without it. 9% sounds bad until you know chance is 3%.

Checkpoint

A saved snapshot of a model partway through training.

We save the checkpoint that scored best on held-out data, rather than whichever happened to be last.

Contrastive loss

A training objective that pulls matching pairs together and pushes non-matching pairs apart, rather than asking the model to hit an exact target.

We switched to this because the old objective could be satisfied by predicting the same average answer for everything.

Covariance

A measure of how two signals move together. Positive if they rise and fall in step, negative if they move oppositely.

For EEG, the pattern of covariance between electrodes is often more informative than any single electrode.

Cross-attention

The mechanism inside an image generator that lets an external signal influence what gets drawn. Normally fed a text prompt; here it is fed EEG.

Early stopping

Halting training when performance on held-out data stops improving, instead of running a fixed number of rounds.

Prevents the model from continuing to memorise the training set long after it has stopped learning anything general.

Epoch

One complete pass through the training data. Confusingly, in EEG research "epoch" can also mean a segment of a recording — context decides.

Hertz (Hz)

Cycles per second. 10 Hz means the signal completes ten up-and-down cycles every second.

Latent space

A compressed numerical representation. The diffusion model works in a small latent space rather than on full-size pixels, which makes it far faster.

n-way accuracy

Accuracy when choosing between n options. 2-way means picking between the right answer and one distractor (chance 50%); 33-way means choosing among all 33 (chance 3%).

Reporting the n matters enormously — 60% on 2-way is weak, 60% on 33-way would be extraordinary.

Overfitting / memorisation

When a model learns the specific training examples rather than the general pattern. It scores well on data it has seen and poorly on anything new.

We watched this happen: our alignment objective fell to 0.0001, meaning the model had effectively memorised which answer belonged to each training clip.

Regularisation

Techniques that discourage memorisation — penalising large parameter values, freezing parts of the network, adding noise to the inputs.

Sampling rate

How many measurements per second are taken. Ours is 250 Hz, so a half-second window contains 125 numbers per electrode.

SEM (standard error of the mean)

A measure of how much a measured average would bounce around if you repeated the experiment. Used to judge whether a difference is real.

Our perception result beat its control by 4.3 SEM — far too large to be luck. The imagination result was 0.5 SEM — indistinguishable from nothing.

Shuffled-pairing control

Scoring generated images against deliberately mismatched ground truth. Since the image quality is identical either way, any gap between real pairings and shuffled ones must come from the brain signal.

This is the control that makes our perception result meaningful rather than just "the generator makes nice pictures".

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)

How large the thing you want is compared to everything else. EEG imagery has famously poor SNR.

Stimulus-disjoint split

A test where the images used for testing never appeared in training at all. The strictest protocol available.

Our perception result used this, which is why it is the strongest claim the project has.

Top-1 / Top-5 accuracy

Top-1 asks whether the model's single best guess was right. Top-5 asks whether the right answer appeared anywhere in its top five guesses.

Top-5 is more forgiving and often reveals partial knowledge that top-1 hides.

Trial averaging

Combining several recordings of the same thing before deciding, so random noise cancels out while consistent signal accumulates.

Averaging 40 repeats improves signal-to-noise roughly sixfold. This is why repetitions matter so much — and why having only one recording per picture hurts us so badly.

Validation vs test set

Validation data is held out to make decisions during development (which checkpoint, which settings). Test data is held out to produce the final number and touched only once.

Using test data to make decisions quietly inflates your result — the number stops being an honest estimate of performance on genuinely new data.

Weight decay

A regularisation method that penalises large parameter values, nudging the model toward simpler solutions.

Ours was defined in the config but silently never applied — one of the faults we found and fixed.
