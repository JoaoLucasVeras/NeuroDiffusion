Step 00

Start here

The goal: record someone's brain activity while they imagine a picture, and produce that picture.

That sentence promises more than is physically possible, so here is the honest version straight away. We are not reading a picture out of the brain. We extract a weak hint about the category of what someone is thinking about, dog or car or flower, and hand that hint to an image generator that already knows what dogs look like. Nearly all the visual detail in the output comes from the generator's knowledge, not from the brain.

The one thing to keep in mind while reading everything else

The image generator always produces a good-looking picture, whether or not the brain data means anything at all. So "the output looks like a dog" proves nothing. The only meaningful question is: does the output match the picture the person actually had in mind, more often than it matches some random other picture? Every number in this documentation is built around that question.

Where the project stands, in three lines

  • Seeing works. Feed it EEG recorded while someone looks at an image and the generated pictures match the real stimulus significantly better than chance pairings, on images and a person the model had never seen. The machine is wired correctly.
  • Imagining does not, yet. The same pipeline on imagination EEG comes back at chance. That is a measured result with working controls, not a crash.
  • The data does contain imagery signal. A deliberately simple method finds it at about three times chance. So the remaining problem is ours to fix, not a dead end in the data.

How to read this

The tabs run in order and build on each other. Steps 01 to 03 are the setup: what the signal is, what data we have, how the machine is put together. Steps 04 and 05 are what happened when we ran it. Step 06 is the current state and what is next. The last three tabs are for looking things up rather than reading straight through.

Step 01

The signal: what EEG actually is

In one sentence: an electrode on the scalp measures a voltage that is mostly not brain activity, and the trick is knowing which part is.

An electrode on your scalp measures voltage, the tiny electrical field produced by millions of neurons firing together beneath it. In our data there are 128 of these, each sampled 250 times per second.

That voltage is not "brain activity". It is a sum of several things stacked on top of each other, and several of the non-brain contributions are larger than the brain signal:

What contributesTypical sizeIs it brain?
Brain rhythms (what we want)10–50 µVyes
Eye blinks and eye movement100–200 µVno
Neck and jaw musclevariesno
Heartbeatsmallno
Electrode driftcan be largeno
Mains hum (60 Hz)variesno

That is the basic condition of the field: the thing you want is one of the smaller components of what you record.

Microvolt (µV)

One millionth of a volt. Brain rhythms at the scalp are tens of microvolts.

For scaleAn AA battery is 1.5 volts, roughly fifty thousand times larger.

Frequency is how you pull them apart

Different sources oscillate at different speeds, so you can separate them by frequency: how many times per second the signal cycles up and down. That unit is the hertz (Hz).

below 1 HzDrift, electrodes settling, sweat, temperature. Not brain.
1–4 HzDelta, deep sleep, and where slow artifact lives
4–8 HzTheta, drowsiness, some memory processes
8–13 HzALPHA — the visual cortex idle rhythm. Where imagery lives.
13–30 HzBeta, active concentration, motor control
above 30 HzGamma, often really muscle activity in scalp recordings

Alpha rhythm

Your visual cortex has a resting oscillation near 10 cycles per second. When you see something, or imagine something, that rhythm is modulated in a way that depends on what you are seeing or imagining.

Why we careThree independent studies, including the paper behind our own dataset, found imagined content carried in this band and essentially nowhere else. That is why we filter to 8–13 Hz: it is where theory says the answer is.

Band-pass filter

A processing step that keeps a chosen range of frequencies and discards everything outside it.

ExampleThe bass and treble knobs on a stereo. Band-passing to 8–13 Hz is like turning everything down except one narrow slice of the spectrum. You hear less, but what remains is the part you care about.

Carry this forward

Two ideas from this tab decide almost everything later: alpha is where imagery lives, and drift below 1 Hz is not brain but can be huge. Step 02 shows why that second one is dangerous in our particular dataset.

Step 02

The data, and the trap inside it

In one sentence: we have four people who each imagined 33 pictures exactly once, which is far less data than it looks like and creates a shortcut a model will happily take.

What was recorded

Four people. Each was shown a picture for half a second, then a noise mask, then spent ten seconds imagining that picture with their eyes open. 128 electrodes recorded throughout, at 250 measurements per second. The dataset is from Shimizu and Srinivasan, 2022, and the same four people also did a separate perception task where they simply looked at images.

The single most important fact about this dataset

Each person imagined each picture exactly once. That ten-second recording was later chopped into 20 half-second pieces, so the file looks like it holds 660 examples per person. It holds 33 events, sliced thin. Twenty slices of one event are not twenty measurements.

A comparable modern dataset has around 800 real imagery events per person. We have 33. That ratio governs the ceiling on everything in this documentation.

Window

One of those half-second slices. Ours are non-overlapping and contiguous: they tile the full ten seconds end to end with no shared samples.

Why that detail mattersBecause they do not overlap, gluing windows 0 to 3 back together reconstructs the genuine continuous signal from 0 to 2 seconds. Had they overlapped, concatenating them would have produced a waveform that never actually happened. This is what made one of our experiments possible at all.

The leakage problem

This is the part that makes EEG decoding unusual, and it is the key to understanding every result later.

Electrode drift

Over seconds and minutes the electrical contact between an electrode and your scalp slowly changes: gel settles, skin warms, you shift in the chair. The measured voltage wanders slowly, below 1 Hz. It has nothing to do with thinking.

The problemThat wander is unique to that recording, at that moment, on that person. It is a fingerprint on the data.

Two half-second clips cut from the same ten-second recording share that fingerprint. Clips from a different recording have a completely different one. And a machine learning model will happily learn the fingerprint instead of the thing you wanted it to learn, because the fingerprint is easier.

The smudged lens

Imagine photographing 33 objects, one per day, with a slightly different smudge on your lens each day. Now train a model to answer "which object is this?"

It can learn the smudges rather than the objects. Test it on more photos from those same days and it scores brilliantly. Hand it photographs someone else took and it fails completely, because it never learned what the objects look like.

Each subject imagined each picture exactly once, so there is a perfect one-to-one correspondence:

picture #7  ↔  one 10-second recording  ↔  one unique drift pattern

Which means "identify the recording" and "identify the picture" are the same task. Anything unique to that recording, including drift, posture, or how the cap was sitting, is a perfect predictor of the label. The model never has to decode imagery at all.

Data leakage

When information from the test set reaches the model during training, making its score look better than its real ability.

Worth knowingIn ordinary machine learning, leakage is a mistake you avoid. In EEG it is the default state, and you have to deliberately engineer it out. Several well-known EEG image-decoding papers have been invalidated for exactly this.

Split protocol

The rule deciding which trials are used for training and which are held back for testing. In EEG this choice matters more than almost anything else.

OursWe train on windows 0 to 10 of each recording and test on windows 16 to 19, discarding the ones in between as buffers so no training window sits next to a test window. The original paper split those 20 windows randomly, which put a test window's immediate neighbours, nearly identical signal, into training. That is why their number is higher than ours on the same data.

Step 03

The pipeline: how the machine is built

In one sentence: teach a network what EEG looks like, then use it to replace the text box on an image generator.

01 Stage 1 — teach a network the shape of EEG

A masked autoencoder: hide random chunks of an EEG recording and train a network to fill them back in. No labels needed, because the recording is its own answer key. The point is to learn what EEG normally looks like, so the next stage starts from something better than random.

02 Stage 2 — steer an image generator with brain signal

Take Stable Diffusion, a system that normally turns text into images, and replace the text input with the EEG encoder from Stage 1. The generator's own image knowledge stays frozen; we train only the parts that translate brain signal into something it understands.

Masked autoencoder

A network trained by hiding part of its input and asking it to reconstruct the hidden part. Because the answer is the input itself, it needs no human labelling, so it can learn from large amounts of raw data.

ExampleCover a quarter of a photograph and ask someone to draw the missing region. To do it well they must learn how photographs generally work: edges continue, textures repeat, shadows fall consistently. That understanding is the real product, not the patch.

Diffusion model

An image generator that starts from pure random static and removes noise in small steps until a picture emerges. What it removes at each step can be steered by a conditioning signal, normally a text prompt, here an EEG recording.

ExampleLike watching a badly tuned TV gradually sharpen into a scene. The conditioning signal is what decides which scene it sharpens into.

CLIP embedding

CLIP is a model that converts an image into a list of about 768 numbers capturing its meaning. Two pictures of different dogs land close together in that space; a dog and a bicycle land far apart. We train the EEG encoder to output numbers close to the CLIP embedding of the picture the person was imagining.

ExampleA coordinate system for meaning. "Golden retriever on grass" sits near "labrador in a park" and far from "red sports car". The EEG encoder's job is to land in the right neighbourhood.

Parameters

The adjustable numbers inside a network, tuned during training. More parameters means more flexibility, and more capacity to memorise instead of learn.

Our situationStage 2 adjusts about 85 million parameters using roughly 360 training examples per subject. That is about 235,000 adjustable knobs per example. A student given 360 practice questions and unlimited memory will memorise the answers rather than learn the subject.

The structural tension of the whole project

Step 02 said we have 33 real events per person. This tab says the model has 85 million adjustable parameters. Those two facts are the reason this is hard, and most of what follows is either a consequence of that mismatch or an attempt to work around it.

Step 04

Training results: what the pipeline did

In one sentence: perception decodes clearly and significantly, imagination comes back at chance, and we can prove both statements are measurements rather than accidents.

How we judge a result at all

Since the generator always makes a plausible picture, we score every result against deliberately broken versions of itself.

Shuffled-pairing control

Score the generated images against deliberately mismatched ground truth. Image quality is identical either way, so any gap between real pairings and shuffled ones must come from the brain signal.

And a second controlFeed the pipeline pure noise instead of EEG. If that scores as well as real data, nothing is working.

SEM (standard error of the mean)

A measure of how much a measured average would bounce around if you repeated the experiment. It converts "this number is bigger" into "this number is bigger than the noise in the measurement".

Rule of thumbBelow about 2 SEM, a gap is not worth believing. Above 4, it is hard to explain away as luck.

Result 1 — seeing a picture works

Model 0.5848, shuffled control 0.5484, noise control 0.4761. The gap over the shuffled control is +4.3 SEM.

It names the right category 9.5% of the time out of 40 options against 2.5% chance, and 25.2% out of 10. Stimulus-disjoint: the 173 test images never appeared in training, on a person the model had not seen.

This is the load-bearing result of the project. It says the machine is wired correctly end to end, so every later failure is about the difficulty of imagination rather than a broken pipeline.

Result 2 — imagining a picture does not, yet

Model 0.5581, shuffled control 0.5541, noise control 0.4746. The gap over the control is +0.5 SEM, which is nothing.

Note that the noise control still separates cleanly. The measurement works; imagination simply is not there. This is an honest null, not a bug.

Result 3 — one subject at a time

Pooling all four people made things worse, so we followed the source paper and trained each person separately. Best scores against 3.03% chance, one correct guess in 33:

RunBest scoreReading
Subject 126.3%Encouraging, and provisional for two reasons below
Subject 312.1%Above chance
Subject 27.1%Weak
Subject 43.0%Chance
Subject 1, imagination + perception together6.1%Much worse than 26.3%
All subjects pooled8.8%Superseded by the per-subject pivot

That last-but-one row is the interesting failure. The source paper reports joint training improving a subject from 13.4% to 25.2%. Ours went the other way, and the likely reason is volume: 6,789 perception trials swamp 363 imagination trials, so the model optimises the task it sees most of.

Why every number in the table above says provisional

First, these are best-validation scores, the peak across many training rounds on a lenient measure, which always flatters. The matching test numbers were lost to a bug: four jobs ran at the same time, their output directories were named by timestamp to the second, and they overwrote each other. Fixed since, by putting the job ID in the path, and the checkpoints survived, so the numbers are recoverable.

Second, and more seriously, these runs trained on raw broadband EEG. Step 05 explains why that is a problem. As of 26 September this is no longer a suspicion: the cross-subject test confirmed that broadband's advantage is recording-specific drift, so 26.3% is substantially inflated and the honest figure is unknown until the pipeline is re-run on alpha-band input.

What we fixed along the way

Getting to a trustworthy null took rebuilding the measurement apparatus: a retrieval metric that needs no image sampling, a validation set carved from the training subjects' late windows instead of quietly reusing test data, best-checkpoint selection, early stopping, the weight decay the configuration asked for and the code silently ignored, input augmentation, and an optional contrastive objective. A crash where a saved CPU random-number state was loaded into the GPU generator was fixed too.

Step 05

The classical baseline

In one sentence: we stopped and ran the simplest possible method, which proved the data does contain imagery signal and simultaneously caught the pipeline taking a shortcut.

The problem it exists to solve

Every time the pipeline came back at chance, that single result could have meant four entirely different things:

  1. There is no imagery signal in this EEG. The data is a dead end.
  2. There is signal, but our model is too large. 85 million parameters, 360 examples, so it memorises instead of learning.
  3. There is signal, but the input representation is wrong. Raw broadband samples when the answer lives in one narrow band.
  4. There is signal and the encoder finds it, but the image-generation stage loses it.

All four produce the identical output, "not distinguishable from chance", and each pipeline run costs one to two GPU-days while resolving none of them individually. That is an expensive place to be stuck.

What a classical baseline is

A deliberately simple, well-understood method. No diffusion, no image generation, no learned representation. Its power comes from what it cannot do:

  • It cannot memorise. A logistic regression on 128 features has nowhere to hide an answer key for 33 classes.
  • It has no generation stage, so nothing downstream can lose the signal.
  • It runs in minutes on a CPU, not days on a GPU.

So it isolates exactly one question: is there decodable imagery information in this EEG at all? If yes, explanation (1) is eliminated and every remaining failure is ours, which means fixable. If no, we stop optimising a pipeline pointed at noise.

The three methods

Band power + logistic regression

For each electrode, measure how much energy sits in the alpha band, then fit the simplest possible classifier, a weighted sum of those 128 numbers.

What it testsDirectly: is alpha power informative? If this works, the signal is strong and simple.

Riemannian tangent space

Instead of each electrode separately, measure how electrodes co-vary, which pairs rise and fall together. Those relationships form a mathematical object that does not live in ordinary flat space, so it is mapped onto a flat surface, the "tangent space", before classification.

ExampleMeasure not each musician's volume but which instruments play together. That pattern of coordination is often more informative than the individual levels. This is the standard strong baseline when EEG training data is scarce.

EEGNet

A compact neural network built specifically for EEG, about 2,000 parameters versus our main model's 85 million. It learns its own spatial and temporal filters.

NoteThis is the architecture that reached 75.8% on a modern imagery dataset. It is the most flexible of the three, which turns out to matter a great deal.

The four conditions

We ran all three methods four ways. Each condition answers one question.

AAlpha band, 500 ms windows

Keep only 8–13 Hz. Is there imagery signal in the band theory predicts? This is the real test; the other three are controls around it.

BBroadband, 500 ms windows

Keep everything, 1–50 Hz. Does band-limiting actually help, or was the alpha theory wrong for our data?

CAlpha band, 2-second windows

Glue four adjacent windows together. Is 500 ms too short? At 10 Hz that is only five cycles of the rhythm we are measuring. The imagery literature uses 2 to 4 seconds.

DAlpha band + artifact rejection

Throw away the 10% most extreme trials, mostly blinks. One paper says cleaning hurts decoding, another says it helps in low-signal cases like ours. Cheap enough to settle on our own data.

Condition A — the finding

Eleven of twelve results above chance, across four different people and three independent methods, in the band theory predicted. Top-5 accuracy sat at 21 to 31% against a 15% chance baseline.

Modest. But it says plainly: there is decodable imagery signal in this EEG. Explanation (1) is eliminated.

Condition B — the trap, caught

Broadband roughly doubled every number. That is not extra signal, it is the drift shortcut from Step 02. Three reasons to be confident:

  • It is too good relative to the field. The original paper got 13.4% on 40 options with a leaky split. Subject 1's broadband 37.1% on 33 options would beat everything published.
  • The pattern gives it away. EEGNet, the most flexible method, was the worst on alpha at 2.0× and the best on broadband at 12.2×. When your most powerful model gains most from data that should not matter, it is exploiting a shortcut.
  • The mechanism is known. Broadband keeps everything from 1 Hz up, including slow drift. Band-passing to 8–13 Hz filters that drift away, which is exactly why the number drops.

The lower number is the honest one. Genuinely counterintuitive: removing data made the result worse but more trustworthy. It is also why this field is littered with results that did not replicate.

Confirmed on 26 September, and this was a real test

The reasoning above was an inference until we crossed subjects. Trained on one person and tested on another, broadband scores 1.30× chance and alpha scores 1.22× — the two-to-one advantage broadband held within a subject is completely gone. An advantage that lives inside a recording and dies between recordings is a fingerprint, not content.

The prediction was stated before the job ran, and there was a clearly defined outcome that would have proved it wrong: broadband crossing better than alpha would have meant we were discarding real signal by band-passing. It did not.

Condition C — inconclusive

Subjects 2 and 3 improved, subject 4 did not. But gluing windows into 2-second segments leaves only 99 training and 33 test samples, so these numbers bounce around badly. Suggestive, not settled.

Condition D — cleanly negative, and useful

Artifact rejection came out roughly neutral, sometimes up, sometimes down, no consistent direction. That resolves a disagreement between two papers for our data: crude artifact handling does not matter much here.

A correction, added 26 September

We first read this as closing the artifact-removal question entirely. That was too strong. Condition D tested trial rejection, which removes a blink by discarding the whole half-second containing it. ICA removes the blink and keeps the trial, which is a materially different trade when you only have 363 training trials to begin with. So condition D weakens the case for artifact handling without actually testing ICA, and that line of inquiry is still live.

What the baseline changed

  1. The worst possibility is eliminated. "There is no imagery signal here" is off the table. Every remaining failure points at something we control.
  2. We have a number to beat. About 3× chance from a model with a few hundred parameters. Any complex model that cannot clear that is not earning its complexity.
  3. We found a trap before it cost weeks. Condition B revealed a shortcut large enough to fake a breakthrough. Finding it in a five-minute CPU job rather than after a month of GPU training is the entire point.
  4. We built a reusable instrument. Five CPU-minutes instead of a GPU-week per experiment, and the first thing to run on day one of our own recordings to ask whether the data is any good.
The uncomfortable implication

Our Stage 2 model trains on raw broadband EEG, exactly the condition that just produced up to 12× chance through drift fingerprinting. So subject 1's headline 26.3% may be substantially drift-driven rather than imagery decoding.

Read correctly, this is the baseline doing its job: it found a problem in the expensive pipeline that the expensive pipeline could not find in itself.

Step 06

Where we are, and what is next

In one sentence: forward, decisively, but it is the kind of forward step that makes some earlier numbers look worse.

The project a week ago: an expensive pipeline producing ambiguous nulls, with no way to tell whether the data was the problem. The project now: signal in the data confirmed at p ≤ 0.05 in all four subjects, the drift shortcut measured rather than suspected, a benchmark to beat, and a fast way to test the next idea.

Where the evidence stands after 26 September

Solid: perception decodes at +4.3 SEM on unseen images. Imagery signal exists in the alpha band, significant in all four subjects under a permutation null.

Settled negative: imagery does not transfer between people in this data. Broadband's advantage is recording-specific drift.

Unknown: what the pipeline actually achieves on alpha-band input. Every deep-model number we have was trained on broadband and is provisional until re-run.

The queue, in order

1Alpha-band input to the main pipeline — next up

Stage 2 currently trains on raw broadband, now measured to be the condition that produced up to 12× chance through drift. This is a fix to a confirmed problem rather than a guess at an improvement. One to two GPU-days per subject.

2Recover the four lost test reports

The checkpoints survived the collision and are identifiable by epoch. Cheap re-runs that turn four provisional validation numbers into real test numbers. Worth doing regardless, since the current headline figure cannot properly be cited.

3A small encoder

EEGNet class, a few thousand parameters instead of 85 million. The argument got stronger on 26 September: subject 4 is significant at 3× chance in the baseline while the big model scored exactly chance on the same data. The signal is there and the large model cannot find it.

4ICA artifact removal, as a fifth baseline condition

Condition D tested discarding whole trials, not repairing them. ICA removes a blink and keeps the trial, which matters when there are only 363 of them. Minutes of CPU to settle.

5Shrink the label space

Gao et al.'s headline 75.8% is a three-class result. A working three-class imagery-to-image system is far more likely to function than 33 classes at 3× chance, and 75% on three categories is enough to steer a generator into the right region. Arguably the better plan rather than a fallback.

7The Gao et al. 2026 dataset

22 participants, 10 classes, 40 repetitions per class per session, two sessions. Roughly 800 real imagery events per person against our 33. Second track and validation target.

8Our own recordings

The long game, and the only path past the data ceiling. Hardware first, then the classical baseline on day one to check the protocol before collecting at volume.

The finding survives a permutation test, and the drift diagnosis is confirmed

Three questions in one CPU job, each with a prediction stated in advance. Two came back confirmed, one came back negative.

1. Our headline finding is realPermutation null on the within-subject alpha baseline, 200 shuffles per subject, labels shuffled per recording rather than per window: S1 p=0.005 (3.25× chance), S2 p=0.050 (2.25×), S3 p=0.010 (2.75×), S4 p=0.005 (3.00×). The shuffled distributions centre on 0.0291–0.0308 against a chance of 0.0303, so the test is calibrated and can be trusted. "There is decodable imagery signal in this EEG" is now a defensible claim rather than an encouraging observation. S2 sits exactly on the line at 0.0498, so call that one suggestive.

2. Imagery does not transfer between peopleAll 12 ordered pairs, trained on one person and tested on another over the 33 stimuli they share, using all 20 windows on each side and per-subject label-free alignment. Alpha: mean 1.22× chance, 1 of 12 pairs below p=0.05. Broadband: mean 1.30×, 2 of 12. With twelve tests you expect about 0.6 false positives by luck, and neither survives correction for multiple comparisons. This closes the door on pooling subjects for more data, and confirms the per-subject pivot was the right call.

3. The broadband advantage was driftThis is the discriminating result. Within a subject, broadband beat alpha roughly two to one. Across subjects that entire advantage evaporates: 1.30× against alpha's 1.22×, which is no difference at all. An advantage that exists inside a recording and vanishes between recordings is a fingerprint, not neural content. Predicted in advance, and it held.

ChangedAlpha-band input to the main pipeline moves from speculative to justified, and subject 1's 26.3% is now known to be substantially inflated with a measured mechanism behind it. Also notable: subject 4 is significant at 3.00× here despite scoring exactly at chance in the deep model. The signal is in that subject's data and our 85-million-parameter model could not find it, which is an argument for a small encoder.

Resources: datasets and papers

What this is: everything the team has proposed, with a verdict on each. Tagged by who found it. The full write-ups are in RESOURCES.md in the repository.

The four criteria we judge a dataset by

1. Visual imagery, not perception and not motor imagery. 2. Real repetitions of each imagined item, not one event sliced into windows. 3. Posterior electrode coverage, since that is where visual imagery shows up. 4. More than one session, so a result can be shown to survive a new cap placement.

Datasets

Shimizu & Srinivasan (2022)Joao

Our current dataset. Four subjects, 128 channels, 40 stimuli imagined once each for ten seconds, plus a separate perception task with 2,000 stimuli.

VerdictIn use, and the source of the project's one positive result. Its limit is the hard one: 33 imagery events per person, not the 660 the file appears to hold.

Gao et al. (2026), visual imagery BCIJoao

22 participants, 10 classes, 40 repetitions per class per session, two sessions, 32 channels. Published EEGNet baseline of 75.8% on three classes.

VerdictThe one dataset worth adding. The only candidate that clears all four criteria, and roughly 800 real imagery events per person against our 33.

MindBigData "ImageNet of the Brain"Aditya

Very large by trial count, which is the right instinct, and easy to obtain.

VerdictNot suitable. There are no occipital electrodes, so the region where visual imagery appears is not recorded at all; it is also one subject, and perception rather than imagery. The headline count counts each channel as a separate signal.

OpenNeuro ds007162Aditya

Cortex-wide recurrence in object recognition. Openly licensed and carefully documented.

VerdictNot suitable for the imagery goal, but the best-built dataset anyone has proposed. Perception only, and the task is paperclip objects rather than natural images. Worth keeping as a reference for how to document a dataset properly.

PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/ImageryAditya

109 subjects, a classic and very clean dataset.

VerdictNot suitable. "Imagery" here means imagining moving a hand or foot, which lives in motor cortex at different frequencies. Wrong substrate for visual content.

meagmohit/EEG-Datasets, curated listAditya

The standard community index of public EEG datasets.

VerdictLargely exhausted for our purposes, and that is itself a finding. The list has no visual-imagery category at all, which is a direct measure of how rare this data is and why collecting our own is on the plan.

THINGS-EEG (ds003825)Joao

Large, well-built perception dataset with many repetitions.

VerdictPerception, so not directly useful, but worth keeping in view as a pretraining source for the encoder.

Papers that changed what we do

Xie, Kaiser & Cichy (2020), imagery and perception share neural representationsJoao

Locates imagined content in the alpha band, at posterior electrodes, in a specific time window after the cue.

UsefulYes, the most actionable paper we have. It is why the classical baseline tested alpha first, and why alpha-band input to the pipeline is now a priority.

Stecher & Kaiser (2024), representations of imaginary scenesJoao

Second independent source for the same conclusion, with a concrete time window.

UsefulYes. Two independent papers agreeing is what turned "try alpha" into the default assumption.

Shimizu & Srinivasan (2022), the dataset paperJoao

The result we are measured against: 13.4% on 40 options, rising to 25.2% when imagination and perception are trained jointly.

UsefulYes, centrally, with two caveats we have to carry: their window split was random, which leaks, and their joint-training gain did not reproduce for us.

Kessler, Enge & Skeide (2025), how preprocessing shapes decodingJoao

Finds that aggressive cleaning can remove decodable information along with the artifacts.

UsefulYes, as a policy. Do not add artifact correction hoping for a better number. Condition D of our baseline tested this on our own data and agreed: neutral.

Hajhassani et al., cleaning in low-signal regimesJoao, following up Aditya's batch

The opposite finding to Kessler: cleaning helps when signal is weak.

UsefulYes, and it changed the plan. Two credible papers disagreeing is why we ran both arms rather than picking a side. Aditya's reading list is what surfaced the disagreement.

Li et al. (2024), EEG embeddings and large pretrained modelsJoao

Finds that large pretrained encoders do not automatically win on small EEG datasets.

UsefulYes, as an architecture target, and it supports trying a small encoder before a bigger one.

Riemannian geometry approaches for EEG (2024 review)Joao

The standard strong baseline family when EEG training data is scarce.

UsefulYes, this is the basis of one of the three baseline methods.

Perils and pitfalls of block design for EEG classificationJoao

Shows how presenting one category at a time lets a model learn the block rather than the content.

UsefulYes, as a design constraint. This dictates how our own recording protocol has to be randomised when we collect data.

Papers on artifact removal

Aditya's batch on ICA-based artifact removal: the foundational Jung et al. paper, the Jiang, Bian & Tian review, an ICA template method for blinks, and an ICA denoising study on word imagination. Useful as background rather than as a next step. Our condition D found rejection neutral on this data, so this line is parked rather than dismissed; it becomes relevant again when we record our own sessions and control the noise ourselves.

Glossary

What this is: every technical term used in this documentation, in plain language, alphabetically.

Alpha rhythm

The visual cortex's resting oscillation near 10 cycles per second, in the 8 to 13 Hz band. Modulated by what you see or imagine.

Where the imagery signal lives, according to three independent studies and our own baseline.

Artifact

Anything in an EEG recording that is not brain activity: blinks, muscle tension, heartbeat, electrode drift, mains hum.

Often larger than the brain signal itself.

Band-pass filter

A step that keeps a chosen range of frequencies and discards the rest.

Like turning down every part of a stereo except one narrow slice.

Chance level

The accuracy you would get by guessing randomly. With 33 possible answers that is 1/33 = 3.03%.

Every accuracy number is meaningless without it. 9% sounds bad until you know chance is 3%.

Checkpoint

A saved snapshot of a model partway through training.

We save the one that scored best on held-out data rather than whichever happened to be last.

CLIP embedding

About 768 numbers describing an image's meaning, such that similar images land near each other.

The target the EEG encoder is trained to hit.

Contrastive loss

A training objective that pulls matching pairs together and pushes non-matching pairs apart, rather than asking the model to hit an exact target.

We added this because the old objective could be satisfied by predicting the same average answer for everything.

Covariance

A measure of how two signals move together: positive if they rise and fall in step, negative if they move oppositely.

For EEG, the pattern of covariance between electrodes is often more informative than any single electrode.

Cross-attention

The mechanism inside an image generator that lets an external signal influence what gets drawn. Normally fed a text prompt, here fed EEG.

Diffusion model

An image generator that starts from random static and removes noise step by step until a picture emerges.

Early stopping

Halting training when performance on held-out data stops improving, instead of running a fixed number of rounds.

Prevents a model from continuing to memorise long after it has stopped learning anything general.

EEGNet

A compact neural network designed for EEG, roughly 2,000 parameters.

One of our three baseline methods, and a candidate replacement for the 85-million-parameter encoder.

Electrode drift

Slow wandering of measured voltage, below 1 Hz, caused by the electrode's contact with the scalp changing over time.

Not brain activity, and unique to each recording, which makes it a fingerprint a model can exploit.

Epoch

One complete pass through the training data. Confusingly, in EEG research "epoch" can also mean a segment of a recording; context decides.

Hertz (Hz)

Cycles per second. 10 Hz means the signal completes ten up-and-down cycles every second.

Data leakage

When information from the test set reaches the model during training, making its score better than its real ability.

In EEG this is the default state rather than an occasional mistake.

Latent space

A compressed numerical representation. The diffusion model works in a small latent space rather than on full-size pixels, which makes it far faster.

Masked autoencoder

A network trained by hiding part of its input and reconstructing the hidden part, so it needs no labels.

Microvolt (µV)

One millionth of a volt. Brain rhythms at the scalp are tens of microvolts.

n-way accuracy

Accuracy when choosing between n options. 2-way means the right answer against one distractor, chance 50%; 33-way means choosing among all 33, chance 3%.

Reporting the n matters enormously. 60% on 2-way is weak; 60% on 33-way would be extraordinary.

Overfitting / memorisation

When a model learns the specific training examples rather than the general pattern. It scores well on data it has seen and poorly on anything new.

We watched this happen: our alignment objective fell to 0.0001, meaning the model had effectively memorised which answer belonged to each training clip.

Parameters

The adjustable numbers inside a network. Ours: about 85 million trainable, against roughly 360 training examples per subject.

Permutation test

Shuffling the labels many times to see how often a result as good as yours appears by pure chance.

On our queue, because with 33 stimuli the effective sample size is small enough that the usual assumptions do not hold.

Regularisation

Techniques that discourage memorisation: penalising large parameter values, freezing parts of the network, adding noise to the inputs.

Riemannian tangent space

A way of classifying covariance patterns by mapping them from their own curved space onto a flat one first.

The standard strong baseline when EEG data is scarce, and one of our three methods.

Sampling rate

How many measurements per second are taken. Ours is 250 Hz, so a half-second window holds 125 numbers per electrode.

SEM (standard error of the mean)

How much a measured average would bounce around if the experiment were repeated. Used to judge whether a difference is real.

Our perception result beat its control by 4.3 SEM, too large to be luck. The imagination result was 0.5 SEM, indistinguishable from nothing.

Shuffled-pairing control

Scoring generated images against deliberately mismatched ground truth. Since image quality is identical either way, any gap must come from the brain signal.

The control that makes our perception result meaningful rather than just "the generator makes nice pictures".

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)

How large the thing you want is compared to everything else. EEG imagery has famously poor SNR.

Split protocol

The rule deciding which trials train the model and which test it.

In EEG this choice affects the final number more than almost any modelling decision.

Stimulus-disjoint split

A test where the images used for testing never appeared in training at all. The strictest protocol available.

Our perception result used this, which is why it is the strongest claim the project has.

Top-1 / Top-5 accuracy

Top-1 asks whether the model's single best guess was right. Top-5 asks whether the right answer appeared anywhere in its top five.

Top-5 is more forgiving and often reveals partial knowledge that top-1 hides.

Trial averaging

Combining several recordings of the same thing before deciding, so random noise cancels while consistent signal accumulates.

Averaging 40 repeats improves signal-to-noise roughly sixfold. This is why repetitions matter, and why one recording per picture hurts us badly.

Validation vs test set

Validation data is held out to make decisions during development. Test data is held out to produce the final number and touched once.

Using test data to make decisions quietly inflates your result.

Weight decay

A regularisation method that penalises large parameter values, nudging a model toward simpler solutions.

Ours was defined in the config but silently never applied, one of the faults we found and fixed.

Window

One half-second slice of a recording. Ours are non-overlapping and tile the full ten seconds end to end.

Twenty windows of one imagery event are not twenty measurements of it.
